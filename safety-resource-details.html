Emergency planning for real buildings.

Why the best fire plan is the one your people can remember at 2am — and how to build one that survives contact with reality.

Most emergency plans fail for a mundane reason: they were written for a building as designed, not the building as used. Doors get propped open, storage migrates into escape routes and staff rotate faster than the plan is updated.

This guide sets out the approach our engineers use when helping clients build plans that work in practice — short enough to remember, specific enough to act on and robust enough to change as the building changes.

Start with how people actually move

Before drawing anything, walk the building at its busiest and quietest times. Note where people genuinely go, which exits they favour and where they hesitate, then formalise the routes people already trust.

"A plan that depends on people reading a notice in an emergency is not a plan. It is a hope."

    Make instructions impossible to misread

    Use short, imperative sentences and place them where the decision happens: beside the call point, at the exit and near the assembly point.

    • Identify two routes from every occupied area
    • Name a responsible person and deputy per floor
    • Show the assembly point physically
    • State who calls the fire service and what to say

    Design for the first sixty seconds

    Train for the first minute specifically. People should know the nearest call point without thinking, understand not to investigate a fire, and know that closing a door protects the corridor behind them.

      Practical note

      Only tackle a fire if trained, the fire is small, escape is clear behind you and the correct extinguisher is to hand. In every other case, get out and raise the alarm.

      Practise, then change the plan

      A drill is the only reliable way to discover that the assembly point floods or a marshal is stationed behind a secured door. Time it, record surprises and issue a one-page change note.

      • Announce a drill without warning the marshals
      • Time the full evacuation to assembly
      • Ask three people to explain what they did
      • Update the plan within seven days

      Keep the evidence

      Insurers and authorities want to see that the plan is live. Keep dated versions, drill records, training attendance and remedial actions together in one maintained place.

        • Write for people under stress, not for a filing cabinet.
