Emergency planning for real buildings.

Why the best fire plan is the one your people can remember at 2am — and how to build one that survives contact with reality.

Most emergency plans fail for a mundane reason: they were written for a building as designed, not the building as used. Doors get propped open, storage migrates into escape routes and staff rotate faster than the plan is updated. A good plan accounts for all of it.

This guide sets out the approach our engineers use when helping clients build plans that work in practice — short enough to remember, specific enough to act on and robust enough to change as the building changes.

Start with how people actually move

Before drawing anything, walk the building at the busiest time and again at its quietest. Note where people genuinely go, which exits they favour and where they hesitate. The routes people already use are usually the safest ones to formalise — you are reinforcing instinct rather than fighting it.

"A plan that depends on people reading a notice in an emergency is not a plan. It is a hope."

Make instructions impossible to misread

Use short, imperative sentences. Place them where the decision happens: beside the call point, at the exit, near the assembly point. Avoid floor-plan diagrams with tiny type that nobody can read in low light. A single line — "Go left to the stairwell, do not use the lift" — outperforms a page of procedure.

  • Identify two routes from every occupied area wherever possible.
  • Name a responsible person for each floor, with a named deputy.
  • Show the assembly point physically, not just in the handbook.
  • State clearly who calls the fire service and what information to give.

Design for the first sixty seconds

The first minute determines the outcome more than any later action. Train for it specifically. People should know the location of the nearest call point without thinking, understand that they should not investigate a fire themselves, and know that closing a door behind them can protect an entire corridor.

Practical note

Only attempt to tackle a fire if you have been trained, the fire is small, you have a clear escape route behind you and the correct extinguisher is to hand. In every other case, get out and raise the alarm.

Practise, then change the plan

A drill is not a box-ticking exercise. It is the only reliable way to discover that the designated assembly point floods at high tide, or that a marshal's desk is on the opposite side of a secured door. Time the drill, record what surprised you and issue a one-page change note afterwards.

A useful drill sequence

  1. Announce a drill without warning the marshals.
  2. Time the full evacuation from alarm to assembly.
  3. Ask three people to explain what they did and why.
  4. Record every point of confusion, however small.
  5. Update the plan within seven days and tell everyone what changed.

Keep the evidence

Insurers and authorities will want to see that the plan is live. Keep dated versions, drill records, training attendance and any remedial actions in one place. Our compliance portal does this automatically, but a simple folder works just as well if it is maintained consistently.

Key takeaways

  • Write for people under stress, not for a filing cabinet.
  • Place instructions where decisions happen.
  • Drills are for discovering problems, not proving perfection.
